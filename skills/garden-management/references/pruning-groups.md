# Cutting (pruning) — groups, timings, technique

Two ways in: the RHS group filed on the plant, or the *outcome* the plant flowers/grows from.
If the two disagree, trust the plant's recorded `prune_method` first, then the RHS guidance for
that species — and **verify by search** when the entry is not certain. Never write a group into the
registry you have not confirmed; leave `prune_group` empty and fill `prune_method` instead.

## The RHS group framework (working summary)

| Group | What it is | When to prune | How |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | No routine pruning needed (e.g. *Aucuba*, *Skimmia*, many slow evergreens) | — | Remove dead/damaged wood only |
| 2 | Grown for decorative winter stems (*Cornus alba*, *Salix*, *Rubus cockburnianus*) | Late winter–early spring, before growth | Stool hard: all stems to 5–7 cm every 1–3 years |
| 3 | Grown for foliage effect (*Buddleja davidii*, *Cotinus*, *Sambucus*, *Eucalyptus*) | Early spring as buds break | Cut to a low framework or to the ground for big leaves |
| 4 | Evergreen shrubs | Late spring–early summer, after frost risk | Trim to shape; never into old bare wood |
| 5 | Flower on the current year's growth (late-summer/autumn flowerers, *Caryopteris*, *Hydrangea paniculata*, shrub roses) | Late winter–early spring | Cut back the previous year's growth, leave a framework |
| 6 | Flower on the previous year's wood, mid-season (*Philadelphus*, *Weigela*, *Deutzia*, *Forsythia*) | Immediately after flowering | Thin and shorten flowered stems; remove a share of old wood |
| 7 | As 6 but larger, and early-flowering (*Ribes*, *Kerria*, *Choisya* in mild areas) | Straight after flowering | Shorten flowered shoots, remove oldest stems at the base |
| 8 | Shrubs that need restricting or renovating after flowering | After flowering | Varies: cut back to a low framework, take out old wood over 2–3 years |
| 9 | Wall-trained ornamentals flowering on previous year's wood (pyracantha, ceanothus, *Fremontodendron*) | After flowering | Spur back to the wall, tie in framework |
| 10 | Slow-growing shrubs needing little (many small evergreens, hebes) | Late spring | Light trim to shape |
| 11 | Restricting growth / formal hedges and screens | Late spring–summer, as needed | Trim to a line, slightly battered (wider at the base) |
| 12 | Wall shrubs/hedges with large leaves grown for foliage | Spring | Cut back hard every few years |
| 13 | Specialist / best-seen plants (*Acer*, *Hamamelis*, climbing hydrangea, *Wisteria*, hydrangeas) | See the entries below | Plant-specific — do not generalise |

Groups are a starting point, not a law. If a plant's group number is uncertain, search
"RHS prune <botanical name>" before acting, then record the result on the plant row.

## The main cases by outcome

**Spring-flowering shrubs** (*Forsythia*, *Philadelphus*, *Weigela*, *Deutzia*, *Ribes*, flowering
currants, *Kerria*): prune immediately after flowering — this year's growth carries next year's
flowers. Cutting in winter removes the flowers.

**Summer/autumn-flowering shrubs** (*Buddleja davidii*, *Caryopteris*, *Hydrangea paniculata*,
*Perovskia*, *Ceratostigma*): prune late winter/early spring; they flower on new wood.

**Wisteria**: twice a year. Shorten long shoots to ~15 cm in late summer (July–August), then cut
back to 2–3 buds in January/February. Don't cut the main framework.

**Climbing roses / ramblers**: ramblers (one flush) — prune after flowering, tie in new canes and
take out old ones at the base. Repeat-flowering climbers — January/February, train stems
horizontally (that is what makes flowers), shorten side shoots to 2–3 buds.

**Shrub (bush) roses**: winter (January–March; later in cold areas). Remove the oldest third of
stems at the base, take out crossing/rubbing growth, shorten remaining stems by about a third to
a half — to an outward-facing bud on bushy varieties, to a low permanent framework on
hybrid teas. Never cut into the bud union. Deadhead continuously through summer (but leave hips
on species roses if you want the hips and birds).

**Lavender**: trim immediately after flowering (usually mid-August) — flowered stems back to
just above the leafy growth, never into the old bare wood; a yearly tidy keeps it from going
leggy.

**Hydrangeas**: *H. macrophylla* (mopheads/lacecaps) — don't prune; deadhead and only remove old
stems in spring, and know that a hard winter may kill the flower buds. *H. paniculata* — hard in
early spring. *H. arborescens* ('Annabelle') — cut to the ground in spring. *H. quercifolia* —
minimal, after flowering.

**Deciduous shrubs for coloured stems** (*Cornus*, *Salix*): stool hard in late winter/early
spring for fresh coloured growth; leave the best stems for winter colour if you use them in
arrangements.

**Herbaceous perennials**: cut back in autumn for tidiness or leave seedheads/top growth for
wildlife and structure until late winter/early spring, then cut down before growth appears.
Early-flowering perennials (some geraniums, delphiniums) get a mid-season cut after their first
flush for a second round. Divide congested clumps as growth starts (spring) or right after
flowering.

**Ornamental grasses**: evergreen grasses — comb out dead growth in spring, don't cut. Deciduous
grasses — cut to the ground in late winter/early spring just before growth starts (earlier in
mild areas, and never in mild wet weather when the crown can rot).

**Hedging**: formal *Buxus*/*Taxus*/*Ilex* — trim late spring/summer, last cut by the end of
August. Beech/hornbeam — cut in late summer, and hold the winter leaves. **Never cut a hedge
while birds are nesting (roughly March–August)**; check before every cut.

**Trees**: structural pruning in dormancy for deciduous trees (avoid *Prunus*, *Acer*, *Betula*,
*Juglans*, *Vitis* which bleed — they are best pruned in late summer). Remove dead, diseased,
damaged and crossing branches, and keep the leader structure clear. Never cut into the branch
collar; anything above head height or over property with doubt — recommend a qualified arborist.

**Acers (*Acer palmatum*)**: minimally, late summer/early spring at the latest, no heavy cuts;
they bleed and are prone to wound infection. Leaf scorch is wind/sun/drought, not a disease.

**Evergreens**: prune in late spring/early summer once frosts are past — hard cutting into old,
bare wood will not regenerate on conifers.

**Ferns / ornamental wild areas**: remove dead fronds after the worst of winter, before croziers
unfurl (usually late winter).

## Rules that override every calendar

**Outdoors (`placement='outdoor'`) — weather rules:**
- No pruning in hard frost, snow, or extreme heat — the plant cannot seal the wound.
- No hard pruning in drought or after a shock (drought, flood, disease).
- Anything below about 0 °C, or a forecast drop below 0 °C: wait. Fresh cuts are frost damage.

**Indoors (`placement='indoor'`) — none of the above applies; these do instead:**
- Prune in the growing season (spring/early summer) unless the plant's own routine says otherwise:
  a houseplant cut in the dark months heals slowly and can rot back from the wound.
- Never prune a plant that is already struggling with light, water or pests — fix that first.
- Sharp, clean tools matter more indoors: the same blade on plant after plant spreads sap-borne
  problems in a small collection. Wipe with alcohol between plants.
- Keep the plant out of draughts and away from radiators while it calluses, and stop feeding for a
  couple of weeks after a hard cut.
- Prune for airflow and structure before pruning for size.
- Never remove more than about a third of the canopy in a year on a mature plant.
- Right tools, sharp and clean (disinfect between plants if you suspect disease); the cut is
  slanted just above an outward-facing bud or at the branch collar, and no stub is left.
- Hedge/wildlife (**outdoors only** — there is no nesting season in the living room): nesting
  birds (protected by law in the UK), hibernating hedgehogs in piles and around pond margins, frogs
  under mulch/leaf litter — check before you clear or cut.
- After the cut: record it (`plant update <ref> --pruned-on <date>`) and note the response a few
  weeks later (`obs add --kind growth`). That record is what makes next year's timing better.
